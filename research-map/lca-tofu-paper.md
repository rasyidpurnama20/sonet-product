## Abstract

The tofu industry is one of the most important food-processing sectors in Indonesia, given its high consumption rate and notable nutritional value. However, the environmental impacts of each production stage have not been comprehensively identified. Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) with a gate-to-gate system boundary was applied to evaluate the environmental impacts of tofu production at a small and medium-sized enterprise (SME) in Semarang City. The LCA was conducted using SimaPro 10.3 software with the CML-IA Baseline impact assessment method, covering five impact categories: Global Warming Potential (GWP), Ozone Layer Depletion (ODP), Fresh Water Aquatic Ecotoxicity (FAET), Acidification, and Eutrophication. Water use was assessed using the AWARE method. The results demonstrate that the washing and boiling stages are the primary contributors to environmental impacts. Normalization results indicate that Fresh Water Aquatic Ecotoxicity is the most dominant impact category (1.33 × 10³ kg 1,4-DB eq), followed by Global Warming Potential (5.28 × 10³ kg CO₂ eq). The main environmental hotspots are the organic wastewater from washing, soaking, and coagulation stages, as well as firewood combustion during boiling. Recommended improvement strategies include wastewater treatment systems, water recycling, and fuel substitution. This study provides useful insights for improving environmental performance and supporting sustainable production practices in the tofu industry of Semarang City.

**Keywords:** Life Cycle Assessment; tofu production; CML-IA Baseline; AWARE; environmental hotspot; Semarang

## 1. Introduction

Industrial activity is one of the primary drivers of global economic growth [1]. The tofu industry represents a significant food-processing sector in Indonesia owing to its high consumption rate and notable nutritional value. According to Statistics Indonesia (BPS), average per capita tofu consumption reached approximately 0.163 kg per week in 2024, indicating that tofu remains a staple food in many Indonesian households [2]. This high demand has driven increased production activity, resulting in greater resource and energy consumption, as well as the generation of waste and emissions that may cause environmental pollution if not properly managed [3].

Such impacts are particularly evident in river water quality, as the discharge of industrial wastewater can cause Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) and Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) levels to exceed regulatory standards. In the case of the tofu industry in Mojokerto, for instance, liquid waste was reported to contain BOD levels exceeding 2,000 mg/L and COD levels exceeding 5,000 mg/L — far above the standard thresholds of approximately 50–100 mg/L [4]. Furthermore, the environmental impacts associated with each stage of tofu production have not yet been comprehensively identified. Therefore, a systematic approach such as Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is required to evaluate the full environmental burden of the tofu production process [5].

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is a standardized method used to systematically evaluate the environmental impacts of a product or process based on all input and output flows throughout its life cycle [6]. This method is capable of identifying production stages that constitute environmental hotspots, thereby serving as the scientific basis for developing targeted environmental improvement strategies [7]. Previous studies have applied LCA to assess the environmental impacts of the tofu industry; however, these studies predominantly focused on climate change impact categories and thus failed to comprehensively evaluate other environmental concerns, such as water pollution loads, water scarcity, and solid waste generation across all production stages [8, 9].

Therefore, this study aims to analyze the environmental impacts of tofu production in Semarang City using an LCA approach with a gate-to-gate system boundary — encompassing all stages from washing to packaging — in order to identify environmental hotspots and formulate more sustainable improvement strategies.

## 2. Methods

This research utilized a quantitative methodology based on Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) to assess the environmental impacts of tofu production at a small and medium-sized enterprise (SME) in Semarang City. The evaluation was performed in compliance with **SNI ISO 14040:2016** and **SNI ISO 14044:2016**, comprising four sequential phases: (1) goal and scope definition, (2) life cycle inventory analysis, (3) life cycle impact assessment, and (4) interpretation. Primary data were obtained through direct field measurements, structured interviews with the facility operator, and on-site documentation during a complete production batch. Secondary data were sourced from peer-reviewed literature and the **Ecoinvent v3** database within SimaPro 10.3 software [6].

### 2.1 Goal and Scope Definition

This study aims to assess the environmental impacts of tofu production at the facility level, focusing on water use, energy consumption, and waste generation at each production stage. The **functional unit** was established as **1 kg of tofu produced**, consistent with functional units used in comparable LCA studies of tofu and soy-based food processing. All inventory data were initially collected on a per-batch basis (one full production day), with 1,400 kg of soybeans as raw material input yielding 3,681 kg of tofu.

The **system boundary** adopts a gate-to-gate approach, encompassing all unit processes from the initial washing of soybeans to final molding and packaging (Figure 1). Upstream operations (soybean farming, transportation) and downstream activities (distribution, consumption, end-of-life) were excluded, consistent with prior gate-to-gate LCA investigations of similar Indonesian tofu SMEs [8, 9].

> **Figure 1.** Gate-to-gate system boundary of tofu production life cycle assessment *(washing → soaking → grinding → boiling → filtration → coagulation → molding & packaging)*.

### 2.3 Life Cycle Impact Assessment (LCIA)

The LCIA phase evaluates and quantifies the potential environmental impacts generated by the product system based on the compiled inventory data [6]. In this study, the **CML-IA Baseline** method was applied using SimaPro 10.3, covering five impact categories:

| Impact Category | Abbreviation | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Global Warming Potential (100-year) | GWP100a | kg CO₂ eq |
| Ozone Layer Depletion | ODP | kg CFC-11 eq |
| Fresh Water Aquatic Ecotoxicity | FAET | kg 1,4-DB eq |
| Acidification | AP | kg SO₂ eq |
| Eutrophication | EP | kg PO₄³⁻ eq |

Water use and potential water scarcity were additionally assessed using the **AWARE (Available WAter REmaining) method** to evaluate water deprivation relative to regional water availability.

### 2.4 Interpretation

The interpretation phase contextualizes LCIA results to identify hotspot impact categories and the production stages contributing the greatest environmental burdens. **Normalization** was subsequently applied using the CML-IA Baseline reference values to compare impact categories expressed in different units on a common dimensionless scale. Interpretation was focused strictly within the gate-to-gate system boundary to ensure results accurately reflect the stages exerting the greatest environmental influence [12].

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Environmental Impact of Tofu Production

The LCIA reveals that tofu production generates environmental impacts across all five assessed categories. The results are summarized in Table 2.

**Table 2. Environmental Impact of Tofu Production per Batch by Production Stage**

| Impact Category | Unit | Total | Washing | Soaking | Grinding | Boiling | Coagulation | Molding & Packing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GWP (100a) | kg CO₂ eq | 5.28 × 10³ | 3.56 × 10³ | 0.945 | 35.8 | 1.63 × 10³ | 55.8 | 5.9 |
| ODP | kg CFC-11 eq | 6.11 × 10⁻⁵ | 5.93 × 10⁻⁵ | 2.1 × 10⁻⁹ | 7.93 × 10⁻⁸ | 1.07 × 10⁻⁶ | 5.02 × 10⁻⁷ | 1.29 × 10⁻⁷ |
| FAET | kg 1,4-DB eq | 1.33 × 10³ | 1.22 × 10³ | 0.651 | 24.6 | 70.6 | 14.1 | 2.75 |
| Acidification | kg SO₂ eq | 4.59 | 3.53 | 0.00245 | 0.0925 | 0.723 | 0.226 | 0.0176 |
| Eutrophication | kg PO₄³⁻ eq | 9.10 | 8.30 | 0.0034 | 0.128 | 0.409 | 0.250 | 0.00838 |

> **Note:** The filtration stage does not appear in the impact results because no direct energy inputs or emissions were entered in the inventory model for this stage; it therefore generates no characterization values in SimaPro.

#### 3.1.1 Global Warming Potential (GWP)

Total GWP amounts to **5.28 × 10³ kg CO₂ eq**. The washing stage is the largest contributor at 3.56 × 10³ kg CO₂ eq (~67.4%), followed by the boiling stage at 1.63 × 10³ kg CO₂ eq (~30.9%). The dominance of the washing stage is attributed to large water volumes consumed, which indirectly require energy for water supply and distribution, generating greenhouse gas emissions. In addition, organic-laden washing wastewater has the potential to produce methane (CH₄) if discharged untreated. The boiling stage contributes significantly due to firewood combustion, which releases CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O — all potent greenhouse gases.

#### 3.1.2 Ozone Layer Depletion (ODP)

Total ODP registers **6.11 × 10⁻⁵ kg CFC-11 eq**, with the washing stage dominating at 5.93 × 10⁻⁵ kg CFC-11 eq (~97.1%). This impact is associated not with the water itself, but with the energy-intensive infrastructure supporting water supply and distribution within the production chain, which generates indirect emissions linked to ozone depletion.

#### 3.1.3 Fresh Water Aquatic Ecotoxicity (FAET)

Total FAET reaches **1.33 × 10³ kg 1,4-DB eq**, with the washing stage as the primary contributor at 1.22 × 10³ kg 1,4-DB eq (~91.7%). The large volumes of organic-laden wastewater from soybean washing — containing residual organic matter, dissolved solids, and impurities — constitute a significant toxicity threat to freshwater organisms if discharged without treatment. The boiling stage contributes 70.6 kg 1,4-DB eq (~5.3%) through atmospheric deposition of combustion byproducts (NOₓ, SOₓ) into aquatic environments.

#### 3.1.4 Acidification

Total Acidification impact is **4.59 kg SO₂ eq**. The washing stage accounts for 3.53 kg SO₂ eq (~76.9%), linked to SO₂ and NOₓ emissions from energy generation supporting water provision. The boiling stage contributes 0.723 kg SO₂ eq (~15.8%) through firewood combustion, which produces SO₂ and NOₓ that may form acid rain, potentially degrading soil and water quality.

#### 3.1.5 Eutrophication

Total Eutrophication impact is **9.10 kg PO₄³⁻ eq**, with the washing stage dominant at 8.30 kg PO₄³⁻ eq (~91.2%). This is driven by nutrient-rich washing wastewater containing nitrogen and phosphorus compounds derived from soybean residues. Discharge of these nutrients into water bodies can trigger excessive algal proliferation, depleting dissolved oxygen and disrupting aquatic ecosystems. The boiling stage contributes 0.409 kg PO₄³⁻ eq (~4.5%) through NOₓ emissions deposited via atmospheric pathways.

### 3.2 Water Use Assessment (AWARE Method)

Water use was assessed using the AWARE method to determine potential water scarcity (water deprivation) across all production stages. Total water scarcity impact is **515 m³ world eq** for the entire production system.

**Table 3. Water Use Assessment Results (AWARE Method)**

| Production Stage | Water Scarcity (m³ world eq) | Share (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling | 294 | 57.09 |
| Grinding | 101 | 19.61 |
| Soaking | 39.3 | 7.63 |
| Coagulation | 38.2 | 7.42 |
| Washing | 35.8 | 6.95 |
| Molding & Packaging | 7.63 | 1.48 |
| **Total** | **515** | **100** |

The boiling stage is the dominant contributor (~57.1%), reflecting both direct water consumption for cooking soybean slurry and the substantial upstream water demand associated with firewood supply chains. The grinding stage contributes the second largest share (~19.6%), as continuous water addition is required to achieve appropriate slurry consistency. Within the AWARE framework, the environmental burden is determined not solely by volumetric water use, but also by the upstream energy system and supply chain characteristics of each production stage.

### 3.3 Normalization

Normalization was performed using the CML-IA Baseline reference values to place all impact categories on a common dimensionless scale, enabling direct comparison of relative environmental significance.

**Table 4. Normalization Results of Tofu Production Environmental Impacts**

| Impact Category | Normalized Value | Rank |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh Water Aquatic Ecotoxicity (FAET) | 2.57 × 10⁻⁹ | **1** |
| Global Warming Potential (GWP) | 1.05 × 10⁻⁹ | 2 |
| Eutrophication | 6.89 × 10⁻¹⁰ | 3 |
| Acidification | 1.63 × 10⁻¹⁰ | 4 |
| Ozone Layer Depletion (ODP) | 6.84 × 10⁻¹³ | 5 |

Normalization confirms that **Fresh Water Aquatic Ecotoxicity (FAET) is the dominant environmental impact category** in this production system, indicating that tofu production exerts the greatest pressure on freshwater ecosystems. This finding underscores that liquid waste management — particularly the treatment of organic effluents from washing, soaking, and coagulation — constitutes the most critical environmental challenge in the analyzed production system.

Although GWP frequently receives priority attention in environmental sustainability discussions, its normalized contribution ranks second in this study, indicating that freshwater pollution represents a more acute concern at the facility scale assessed. Eutrophication, which ranks third, further reinforces the urgency of addressing nutrient-laden wastewater streams.

### 3.4 Hotspot Identification and Improvement Strategies

Based on the integrated LCIA and normalization results, two primary environmental hotspots are identified:

**Hotspot 1: Organic Liquid Waste (Washing, Soaking, Coagulation Stages)**
The organic-laden wastewater streams from washing, soaking, and coagulation (whey) are the principal drivers of FAET and Eutrophication, which collectively rank first and third in normalized impact. These effluents — characterized by high BOD, COD, and nutrient loads — require structured management interventions.

Recommended strategies:
- **Anaerobic treatment systems** (biodigester/IPAL biogas) to reduce BOD/COD while recovering biogas energy
- **Constructed wetlands or biofilter systems** for nutrient removal prior to effluent discharge
- **Water recycling and recirculation** within washing and soaking stages to reduce volumetric discharge
- **Whey valorization** (e.g., as animal feed or fermentation substrate) to divert high-nutrient streams from wastewater systems

**Hotspot 2: Firewood Combustion (Boiling Stage)**
The boiling stage is the dominant contributor to GWP, AWARE water scarcity, and the second-largest contributor to acidification and eutrophication. The combustion of 800 kg firewood per production batch releases 1,397 kg CO₂, 0.0499 kg CH₄, and 0.3744 kg N₂O.

Recommended strategies:
- **Fuel substitution** to liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), biomethane from on-site biodigesters, or high-efficiency biomass systems
- **Improved boiler or insulated boiling tank systems** to minimize heat loss and reduce fuel consumption per batch
- **Waste heat recovery** from boiling exhaust to pre-heat water in upstream stages

The LCA-based analysis demonstrates that improvements targeting liquid waste management at the source yield greater environmental reduction benefits than end-of-pipe interventions. Prioritizing these two hotspots is expected to substantially reduce the overall environmental footprint of tofu production in Semarang City.

---

## 4. Conclusion

Based on the Life Cycle Assessment of tofu production in Semarang City using a gate-to-gate approach, the following conclusions are drawn:

1. **Tofu production generates measurable environmental impacts** across all five assessed categories: GWP (5.28 × 10³ kg CO₂ eq), ODP (6.11 × 10⁻⁵ kg CFC-11 eq), FAET (1.33 × 10³ kg 1,4-DB eq), Acidification (4.59 kg SO₂ eq), and Eutrophication (9.10 kg PO₄³⁻ eq).

2. **The washing and boiling stages are the primary contributors** to environmental impact across all categories, driven by high water consumption and firewood combustion, respectively.

3. **Fresh Water Aquatic Ecotoxicity (FAET) is the dominant impact category** based on normalization results (2.57 × 10⁻⁹), confirming that organic liquid waste — particularly washing effluents, whey, and coagulation waste — constitutes the most critical environmental hotspot.

4. **Water scarcity** (AWARE method) totals 515 m³ world eq, with the boiling stage as the dominant contributor (57.1%), largely driven by the upstream water demand of firewood supply chains.

5. **Improvement strategies** should prioritize: (a) implementation of wastewater treatment and recycling systems for washing, soaking, and coagulation effluents; and (b) substitution of firewood with cleaner fuel alternatives to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and water scarcity impacts.

The findings of this study provide actionable, evidence-based guidance for improving the environmental sustainability of SME-scale tofu production and can inform environmental management policies for the tofu industry in Semarang City and Central Java.
