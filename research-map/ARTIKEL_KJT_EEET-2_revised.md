## 1. INTRODUCTION



Aquaculture plays an increasingly essential role in global food security, having surpassed capture fisheries in total production and continued expanding rapidly across coastal regions of Asia (FAO, 2024). This rapid expansion has raised concerns about the environmental performance of open-water aquaculture systems, particularly owing to their reliance on fossil fuels and material-intensive infrastructure that contribute to greenhouse gas emissions and fossil resource depletion (Rossi et al., 2024; Zoli et al., 2024). Life cycle assessment (LCA) has therefore emerged as a critical tool for quantifying environmental burdens and supporting sustainable planning in aquaculture (Ghamkhar et al., 2021).

Although LCA applications in aquaculture are growing, most studies focus on temperate, high-value species such as salmon, seabass, and shrimp and are geographically concentrated in Europe, China, and the Americas (Bohnes et al., 2019). Only a limited number of LCA studies have examined tropical systems in Southeast Asia, such as tilapia farming in Malaysia and Thailand (Chairat et al., 2025; Dullah et al., 2020). These studies consistently identify feed, fuel, and cage materials as key environmental hotspots. Nevertheless, no study has assessed milkfish (*Chanos chanos*) grow-out in coastal net-cage systems, despite the species' considerable importance in regional aquaculture (Alimorin, 2025; Tahiluddin et al., 2025). Existing milkfish LCA research generally addresses pond systems or fingerling production, leaving the environmental profile of coastal grow-out operations unquantified (Pianjing & Wites, 2021; Supartono et al., 2015).

A second knowledge gap concerns production-system coverage. While floating net cages and land-based ponds are well-represented in the LCA literature (Henriksson et al., 2012; Pagnotta, 2025), staked net cages — which rely on bamboo or wooden stakes and multilayer nylon nets anchored to the seabed — have not been evaluated. A third gap is geographical representativeness. Most cage-based LCAs originate from non-tropical regions (Bohnes et al., 2019; Henriksson et al., 2012), whereas tropical coastal environments such as northern Semarang experience tidal flooding, land subsidence, and salinity fluctuations that substantially affect fuel consumption, maintenance frequency, and production yields (Dalimunthe et al., 2025; Romadhona & Arifandi, 2020). Consequently, environmental metrics derived from temperate systems cannot be reliably extrapolated to Indonesian coastal aquaculture.

A fourth gap is the imbalance in reported environmental drivers between feed-related and infrastructure-related impacts. Many aquaculture LCAs identify feed as the dominant contributor to environmental burdens (Henriksson et al., 2012). However, recent studies demonstrate that nylon nets, ropes, and cage structures can contribute equally or more to certain impact categories, particularly when replacement cycles are short (Rossi et al., 2024). The life cycle implications of these infrastructure materials remain poorly characterised in small-scale tropical systems.

Taken together, these four gaps define a clear knowledge deficit that this study addresses. Accordingly, this research performs an attributional, gate-to-gate LCA of milkfish grown in staked net cages in coastal Semarang, using primary field data and the ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint characterisation method, to identify environmental hotspots and support more sustainable coastal aquaculture management.

## 2. METHODOLOGY

### 2.1 Goal and Scope Definition

This study was conducted in accordance with the ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 standards for life cycle assessment. The goal of this study is to quantify the environmental impacts of milkfish (*Chanos chanos*) production in staked net cages in the coastal area of Semarang and to identify the major environmental hotspots. The functional unit (FU) is **1 tonne of live milkfish at the farm gate**. A gate-to-gate system boundary was applied to isolate farm-level production impacts, covering five sequential process stages: site preparation, stocking, nursery, grow-out, and harvesting, as illustrated in Figure 1. Upstream background processes (including material manufacturing and fuel production) were modelled using datasets from the ecoinvent database.

Any material or energy flow contributing less than 1% of the total mass or energy input was excluded unless it was known to carry substantial environmental relevance. This cut-off rule is consistent with ISO 14044 guidance and is commonly applied in aquaculture LCA work to balance inventory completeness with analytical practicality (Jouannais et al., 2023).

### 2.2 System Description

The study was conducted in Tanjung Mas Village, Semarang City, Central Java, Indonesia (Figure 2). This low-lying coastal sector of the Java Sea is strongly influenced by semi-diurnal tides and the seasonal Asian–Australian monsoon system, which drives marked variability in rainfall, currents, and surface salinity (Adyasari et al., 2021). The study location in northern Semarang is further subject to tidal flooding and ongoing land subsidence, both of which affect fuel consumption patterns and maintenance requirements (Adnan et al., 2025; Dalimunthe et al., 2025).

*Figure 2. Location map of the staked net cage aquaculture area in North Semarang.*

Milkfish in this coastal area are cultured in staked net cages constructed from bamboo stakes driven into the muddy seabed, forming rectangular enclosures covering approximately 1 ha of water surface at a depth of 3 m. Three-layer polyamide (nylon-6) nets are deployed, typically with 0.5 cm mesh for the nursery phase and 1.0 cm mesh for grow-out, requiring approximately five rolls (30 × 5 m each) per cage module (Figure 3). The open cage structure promotes natural water exchange and the availability of supplementary natural food. Nets are cleaned periodically to reduce biofouling and are replaced at intervals of approximately every two to three production cycles. Each production cycle spans 10 months, from the stocking of hatchery fry (2–3 cm) through to harvest at a target size of 250–350 g, with daily operational routines of manual feeding, net inspection, and patrol using small gasoline-powered boats.

*Figure 3. Detailed design of the staked net cage used for milkfish cultivation: (A) existing cage in situ; (B) upper view; (C) construction design; (D) material specification; (E) net position on the seabed.*

The collected data encompassed net mass and composition, net replacement cycles, bamboo stake quantities, gasoline consumption per operational stage, feed inputs by type and quantity, production cycle duration, and recorded survival rates. All input and output flows were subsequently normalised to the functional unit.

### 2.4 Life Cycle Impact Assessment

Environmental impacts were calculated using the **ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint (H)** characterisation method, selected for its broad adoption in recent aquaculture LCA studies and its ability to capture mid-point indicators of direct relevance to coastal fish farming operations. Five impact categories were assessed: global warming (GW, kg CO₂ eq), fossil resource scarcity (FRS, kg oil eq), terrestrial acidification (TA, kg SO₂ eq), fine particulate matter formation (PMF, kg PM₂.₅ eq), and ozone formation — terrestrial ecosystems (OF, kg NOₓ eq). Normalisation was subsequently conducted using ReCiPe 2016 World (H) reference values to enable cross-category comparison.

### 3.3 Hotspot Analysis

The characterisation results reveal that the total environmental burden of the staked net cage milkfish system is driven by two primary sources: the nylon-6 net and rope infrastructure installed during site preparation, and the air emissions generated by gasoline combustion across all operational stages. Figure 4 presents the characterisation results graphically.

*Figure 4. Characterisation results by process stage and impact category.*

Nylon-6 (polyamide-6) exhibits high energy and carbon intensity at the point of manufacture, as its production requires high-temperature polymerization and extrusion of petrochemical precursors. Consequently, net and rope components represent a major contributor to the fossil resource scarcity and terrestrial acidification categories through the embodied energy of their production processes. This finding aligns with Rossi et al. (2024), whose LCA of diverse marine aquaculture infrastructures demonstrated that synthetic polymer components frequently constitute the largest single material contributor to resource depletion and acidification impacts, particularly in systems with short net replacement cycles.

Gasoline combustion in boat operations constitutes the second primary driver and is the main mechanism through which grow-out and harvesting generate global warming, fine particulate matter formation, ozone formation, and terrestrial acidification burdens, via CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, NOₓ, and SO₂ emissions. This fuel-based burden accumulates most substantially during grow-out (daily patrol, feeding, and net inspection over 10 months) and harvesting (intensified boat mobilisation). These findings are consistent with De Luca Peña et al. (2024), who reported that the material supply chain for cage infrastructure and subsequent operational fuel use were the two dominant environmental burdens in an offshore multi-use aquaculture–energy system, and with Thomas et al. (2024), who similarly identified farming equipment and vessel operations as primary determinants of several mid-point impact categories in kelp aquaculture LCA.

### 3.5 Mitigation Strategies and Interpretation

Based on the normalisation and hotspot analysis, mitigation efforts should be directed at the two principal environmental drivers identified in this study: nylon-6 net materials and gasoline combustion in boat operations.

For the infrastructure hotspot, practical interventions include extending the service life of nylon-6 nets through scheduled maintenance, regular biofouling removal, and systematic repair and patching programmes, which collectively delay the replacement cycle and reduce the annualised material burden. In parallel, evaluating alternative net materials — including recycled polyamide-6 composites or natural-fibre nets — may offer meaningful reductions in fossil resource scarcity and terrestrial acidification impacts. Pasciucco et al. (2025) demonstrated that recycling polyamide-6 fishing nets into composite materials significantly lowers the environmental footprint of cage infrastructure components relative to virgin nylon production, providing direct evidence for the feasibility and LCA benefit of this strategy.

For the fuel-use hotspot, operational improvements in fuel efficiency can be achieved through route optimisation for daily patrols, scheduled engine maintenance to sustain combustion efficiency, and the gradual adoption of low-sulfur or bio-blended fuels. Longer-term options include transitioning patrol and feeding boats to electric or hybrid propulsion, which, depending on the regional electricity grid mix, could substantially reduce GWP, PM formation, and ozone formation contributions from the operational stages.

The interpretation of the present results is subject to several constraints. First, proxy datasets from ecoinvent v3.8 were used for bamboo-based structural materials and certain feed components, as regionally specific life cycle inventories for tropical aquaculture systems remain limited. Second, the gate-to-gate system boundary excludes hatchery inputs (fingerling production) and all post-farm downstream processes (transport, processing, distribution, and end-of-life), which may influence the magnitude and relative ranking of total environmental burdens if a broader cradle-to-grave perspective were adopted. Third, feed ingredients — particularly the waste-derived wheat flour and bread waste used in this system — were characterised using generic production datasets; the actual upstream burden of these co-products depends on the choice of allocation method applied at the source production system.

## 4. CONCLUSIONS

This study conducted the first attributional, gate-to-gate life cycle assessment of milkfish production in staked net cages in the coastal area of Semarang, Indonesia, providing baseline environmental data for a production system that was previously uncharacterised in the LCA literature. Five production stages were evaluated against five ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint (H) impact categories, supplemented by normalisation against global reference values.

> 🟡 **FIXED — Typos corrected:** "Global waming" → "global warming"; "ovreall" → "overall". Both fixed below.

The results demonstrate that site preparation is the dominant contributor to all assessed impact categories, accounting for 54.6–82% of total burdens, driven predominantly by the manufacture and installation of nylon-6 net and rope infrastructure. Grow-out and harvesting constitute the next most significant stages due to cumulative gasoline combustion across the 10-month production cycle, while stocking contributes negligibly to the overall environmental profile. Among impact categories, normalisation confirms that fossil resource scarcity carries the greatest relative burden (0.140 pt), followed by global warming (0.0545 pt), ozone formation in terrestrial ecosystems (0.0472 pt), terrestrial acidification (0.0236 pt), and fine particulate matter formation (0.0176 pt), a ranking that consistently reflects the system's dual dependence on fossil-derived polymer materials and petroleum-based fuels.

These findings establish two clear and actionable environmental priorities for improving the sustainability of staked net cage aquaculture. Extending the service life of nylon-6 nets through maintenance and repair programmes, and improving fuel efficiency in daily boat operations, represent the highest-leverage interventions available to farmers and policymakers within the existing system configuration. Further research incorporating cradle-to-grave system boundaries, regionally specific inventory datasets for key materials and feed components, and sensitivity analysis of net replacement cycles would enhance both the precision and the broader applicability of these conclusions.
